Six component types, and when to use which

The most common failure in this category is using a modal for something that should have been inline. Each type below has a rule, and the rule is about consequence and focus, not about aesthetics.

TypeUse whenDismissalFocusMobile
Modal An irreversible or financial action needs confirmation, or a decision must be made before continuing. Explicit action only. Not by clicking the backdrop. Trapped. Returns to the trigger on close. Centred, full width minus gutters
Bottom sheet A mobile input needing room, such as picking a time or editing availability. Swipe down, backdrop tap, or Cancel. Trapped while open. The primary form. Becomes a modal on desktop.
Side panel Supporting information the visitor must not leave the page to read. Backdrop, close button, or Escape. Trapped, but low consequence. Slides from the bottom, full height
Inline confirm A low-consequence, reversible action in a list or a row. Acting, or clicking elsewhere. Not trapped. Expands in place
Toast Confirming something that already succeeded. Never for errors needing a decision. Auto after 4 to 6 seconds, or manual. Never steals focus. Announced politely. Bottom, above any sticky bar
Banner A persistent state the user should act on eventually, such as payouts not connected. Only when the state resolves. Not dismissible. In document flow. Top of the content area

What each type looks like on a phone

The difference that matters is not how they look but how much they interrupt. A modal stops everything and should only do that when money or an irreversible action is involved. A banner stops nothing and stays until the state it describes is fixed.

Four rules that override everything else

  1. No modal during checkout. The one exception is a genuine blocking failure, such as the slot being taken while paying. Every other interruption on a payment screen is an abandonment risk.
  2. Anything that moves money gets a modal with the amount in the confirm button. Not "Confirm" but "Refund $40.00". The person should never have to remember what they are agreeing to.
  3. No modal can be the only route to information. Everything in a panel must also exist as a page, because people share links and print things.
  4. Never use a toast for an error the user must act on. A toast that disappears after five seconds is not a way to tell someone their payment failed.

Overlays · Level 2 Customer

Ten overlays. Four of them sit on the booking path, which means four chances to lose a paying customer.

Same content source as the /how-it-works page. Write once, render twice.

Choosing a time slot on the creator profile.
Job
Show the all-in total including the service fee before the customer commits to checkout. This is where the largest documented abandonment cause gets neutralised.
Contains
Creator, date and time in the viewer's zone, duration, price, service fee as a separate line, total, and the refund line. One primary action: Continue to payment.
Copy
Service fee (card processing)  ·  "Free cancellation up to 24 hours before."

Backdrop, swipe, or Back. Returns to availability with the slot still highlighted.
Developer

05

Form rules

Applied to every form in the product. Settle these once rather than deciding them twenty times.

Structure

  • Visible label on every field. Placeholder-as-label fails the moment someone starts typing, and fails permanently for screen readers.
  • Mark optional fields, not required ones. Most fields are required, so marking the minority is less noise.
  • One column. Side-by-side fields break at phone width and cause tab-order confusion.
  • 16px minimum font on inputs, or iOS zooms the viewport on focus.
  • Correct input types and autocomplete attributes, so mobile keyboards and password managers work.

Validation and errors

  • Validate on blur, not on keystroke. Telling someone their email is invalid while they are typing it is hostile.
  • Re-validate on change once a field has errored, so the error clears as soon as it is fixed.
  • Errors sit with their field, programmatically associated, not collected in a summary at the top.
  • Say what happened and what to do. "Enter a valid email address" not "Invalid input".
  • Never clear a form on error. Especially not name and email after a card decline.
  • Disable the submit button only while submitting, never as a validation device.

Every field has to justify itself

Twenty-one percent of checkout abandoners cite a process that was too long. In this product the customer is buying one call from one person, so every field on the checkout is a conversion cost. If a field does not change what we deliver or what the law requires, it does not belong there. Intake questions, where they exist at all, belong after the booking is confirmed, not before the payment.

10

Accessibility requirements

Dialogs and forms are where accessibility failures concentrate. Phase 1 commits to keyboard-navigable booking and checkout with visible focus; these rules deliver that.

Every dialog

  • Marked as a dialog, with its title programmatically associated.
  • Focus moves into the dialog on open, to the heading or the first control, and is trapped while it is open.
  • Focus returns to the trigger on close. The most commonly missed rule, and the most disorienting when absent.
  • Escape closes anything low-consequence. Escape does not close a confirmation for an irreversible action.
  • Content behind the dialog is inert: not scrollable, not reachable by tab.
  • A visible close control, not only a backdrop click.

Every form

  • Label associated with its input. Not a nearby paragraph.
  • Errors programmatically linked to the field and announced when they appear.
  • Required state conveyed in text, never by colour or an asterisk alone.
  • Visible focus on every control, including custom ones like the star rating and the slot picker.
  • The star rating operates as a radio group with arrow keys.
  • Touch targets at least 44 by 44 points, including availability cells.
  • Errors never communicated by colour alone.

Two of these are conversion mechanics, not compliance

Visible focus and errors attached to their field are usually argued on accessibility grounds. In a checkout they are commercial: a customer who cannot tell which field is active, or why a payment was rejected, abandons. Framing them this way keeps them in scope when the schedule tightens.

The shortest useful summary of this document

Modals are for money and for things that cannot be undone. Panels are for doubt. Toasts are for things that already worked. Everything else is inline. And every confirm button that moves money carries the amount, because nobody should have to remember what they are agreeing to.
