01

Executive summary

LINK is a two-sided marketplace where any creator sells paid one-to-one video calls to the audience that already follows them. A working prototype exists. Phase 1 hardens it into a revenue-generating pilot. This report is the research that should determine what gets designed in Weeks 4 to 6 and built in Weeks 7 to 13.

What we learned

The product's hardest problem is not the product. Research on two-sided marketplaces is consistent and unambiguous: marketplaces fail at the cold start, and the companies that survived it all constrained their initial market to reach density faster. Uber, Airbnb and DoorDash all launched city by city. EXTERNAL For LINK this means the single highest-risk decision in the project is not a screen, a flow or a feature. It is how narrowly the first thirty creators are recruited, and that decision is currently unresolved and in disagreement between the parties.

Three of the four gate metrics are decided before a customer ever sees a screen. Creator activation depends on onboarding friction. Call completion depends on reminders. Average rating depends on whether the first cohort is hand-held. Only completed paid calls per week depends meaningfully on the marketplace itself working.

The reminder channel is a measurable risk to a gate metric. Phase 1 ships email-only reminders. Published studies across 29 trials put the no-show reduction from automated reminders at roughly 29 percent on average, with text messages materially outperforming email: email response rates in healthcare settings run 5 to 10 percent against 45 percent for SMS. EXTERNAL Call completion above 80 percent is a Phase 2 gate criterion. Measuring it on the weaker channel puts the gate at risk for reasons unrelated to whether the product is good.

The checkout carries two known, quantified abandonment risks. Baymard's synthesis of 50 studies puts average cart abandonment at 70.2 percent, with 48 percent of abandoners citing unexpected extra costs and 26 percent citing a forced account creation. EXTERNAL LINK's proposed customer service fee is exactly the kind of extra cost that triggers the first, and the decision on guest checkout is still open. Both are design decisions, not engineering ones, and both are cheap to get right now.

The niche decision governs the recruiting list, the intake forms, the price band, the verification requirements and the comparability of early reviews. Leaving it open past Week 3 does not delay one task, it delays five. INFERENCE

Scope is capped at $8,000 to $10,000 by contract, and a bottom-up reading of what remains to be built exceeds the 156 hours the range was priced against. The Week 3 scope-fix is the contractual moment to resolve that, either by cutting depth or by reopening the range if the universal-platform reading is confirmed. INFERENCE

Immediate actions

  1. Settle the launch niche with the ten-names test in the questionnaire, not with further argument. This week.
  2. Decide guest checkout and service-fee disclosure before any checkout screen is drawn.
  3. Add a text-message channel for the one-hour reminder only, or formally restate the call-completion gate.
  4. Confirm prototype access. Every estimate in this report assumes the existing build is available and usable, and that remains unverified.
02

Project context

What exists, what is agreed, and what is still open on 1 October 2026.

The product

An open marketplace where any creator sells live one-to-one calls through a single shareable link, with booking, payment, video and payout built in. Positioned against curated expert networks, shout-out platforms, scheduling tools and membership platforms, none of which combine self-serve access for small creators with an end-to-end booking and payment flow.

The engagement

Phase 1 hardens the existing prototype into a live pilot across thirteen weeks, 91 working days, launching 16 December 2026. Baseline $9,000 inside an $8,000 to $10,000 range, half paid. Phase 2, the full platform, is quoted separately at a gate review four weeks after launch and carries no obligation.

Commercial model

Fifteen percent commission on completed bookings, creators keep eighty-five. A customer-side service fee covering card processing is proposed so the eighty-five is literally true. Funds are held on payment and released after the call completes and the review window passes.

Settled since scope lock

Cancellation policy approved: full refund 24 hours or more ahead, 50 percent inside 24 hours, none inside 2 hours. Three months of post-launch support. Start date, launch date and payment schedule. AI features and call recording confirmed as Phase 2.

Six decisions are still open on day 15 of 91

The launch niche, the social platform connection, call durations and the price band, the service fee approval, the Stripe entity, and the creator cohort. The first of these blocks the other five in whole or in part. The UX questionnaire issued in Week 2 was designed to close them, and at the time of writing the responses are outstanding. PROJECT RECORD

What this report is not

It is not a usability audit of a live product, because the product is not live. It is not a synthesis of user interviews, because none have been conducted. It is not an analytics review, because there is no traffic. Those are real gaps and Section 28 treats them as such. What this report does is establish the strongest defensible position available before design begins, and name precisely what must be tested to move each hypothesis onto firmer ground.

Methodology and evidence system

What was done

  • Document analysis. The full discovery and proposal set, the Milestone 1 scope document and its version 1.1, the delivery schedule, the Service Level Agreement, the scope-lock meeting transcripts of 27 August and 14 and 16 September, and the client correspondence thread from 30 July to 25 September.
  • Secondary research. Marketplace liquidity and cold-start literature, checkout abandonment benchmarks, appointment no-show and reminder-effectiveness studies, creator economy sizing, and the take-rate structures of five comparable platforms.
  • Prior audit review. The structured findings of the earlier screen-by-screen review of the prototype, covering roughly twenty-one screens.
  • Competitive analysis. Positioning and commercial model comparison across direct and adjacent platforms.

What was not done, and why

No user interviews, no usability testing, no survey, no card sorting, no analytics review, and no fresh first-hand inspection of the prototype by the author of this report. The first four were not in Phase 1 scope and are deliberately deferred: the pilot itself is the test. The analytics gap is structural, there is no live product. The last one matters and is stated plainly: the prototype findings in this report are inherited from the earlier project audit, not observed first-hand during this study. They are recorded as prior research, and should be re-verified against the current build before design work depends on them.

Evidence classification used throughout

Every substantive claim carries one of these tags. Where a claim carries none, it is structural description rather than a finding.

  • PRIOR AUDIT and PROJECT RECORD — direct evidence from project files, transcripts, correspondence or the earlier prototype audit.
  • EXTERNAL — supported by published research, industry benchmarks or competitor documentation. Sources in Section 30.
  • OBSERVED — directly verified during this study. Used sparingly, because almost nothing could be.
  • INFERENCE — a reasoned conclusion from the above. Defensible, but not itself measured.
  • HYPOTHESIS — requires validation before it should drive an irreversible decision. Section 25 says how.

Known bias in this study

The author is the delivery party, not an independent assessor. Two specific biases follow. First, an incentive to find that the existing prototype is salvageable, since the commercial model depends on it. Second, an incentive to recommend a narrow launch, since it reduces delivery risk. Both are flagged at the point they arise. The ten-names test proposed for the niche decision is specifically designed to settle that question with the client's own data rather than our argument.

05

Business analysis

The revenue model, examined

Fifteen percent of completed bookings, with processing passed to the customer. On a $40 thirty-minute call the creator nets $34, the platform takes $6 and the customer pays roughly $41.90. INFERENCE

That arithmetic exposes the real business constraint. At $6 per call, the platform needs a large number of completed calls before the revenue is material. The pilot's purpose is therefore not to generate revenue but to establish whether completed calls recur, because recurrence is the only thing that makes the take rate worth anything. INFERENCE This is why "completed paid calls per week, trending up" is the right primary gate metric and why a one-off spike from a marketing blast would be a misleading success signal.

A structural tension worth naming

The client's stated ambition is a universal platform with global expansion, and a sustainable business earning around three million dollars a year in profit. At a fifteen percent take rate on calls of this size, that implies a volume of completed calls in the low tens of millions annually. INFERENCE Nothing about that is impossible, but it is a volume business, and volume businesses are won on repeat usage and distribution rather than on feature breadth. That argues for spending Phase 1 on making one loop work repeatedly, not on making many loops work once.

Where the business is genuinely differentiated

The comparison set splits cleanly. Curated expert networks solve trust by gatekeeping, which excludes the long tail. Shout-out platforms solve payment but sell an asynchronous product, not access. Scheduling and link-in-bio tools serve everyone but handle neither payment custody nor the trust problem. Membership platforms monetise an audience without selling time. EXTERNAL

LINK's position is the intersection nobody occupies: self-serve for small creators and end-to-end for the transaction. That is a real gap. It is also the hardest of the four to execute, because it means solving trust without gatekeeping, which is what verified follower counts are for. INFERENCE

06

Market and industry research

Market size, and the honest caveat

The creator economy is variously sized between roughly $205 billion and $387 billion for 2026 depending on methodology, with one widely cited projection putting it at $323 billion growing at about 26 percent annually. Over 207 million people participate, of whom roughly 50 million are professional or semi-professional. EXTERNAL

What the data does not contain is a figure for paid one-to-one video consultations as a distinct category. It is not broken out by any of the major sizing reports. EXTERNAL Two readings are possible and we cannot distinguish between them from desk research: either the category is too nascent to measure, or it is too small to separate. HYPOTHESIS

This is itself an argument for the pilot structure. A category without reliable sizing data is exactly the case where spending $9,000 to observe real behaviour beats spending $75,000 on a projection. INFERENCE

The most important external finding in this report

The marketplace literature converges on one point with unusual consistency: the cold start, not the product, is what kills two-sided marketplaces, and every major marketplace that survived it constrained its initial market to reach density faster. Uber, Airbnb and DoorDash all launched city by city. Density beats breadth for achieving liquidity. EXTERNAL

The same literature finds that most successful marketplaces end up supply-constrained rather than demand-constrained. For LINK the hard side is creators. That reframes the project: the pilot's job is to prove that creators can be recruited, activated and kept busy enough to stay, and the customer-side experience exists to serve that. INFERENCE

This bears directly on the open niche decision. The client's concern, that a narrow launch pigeonholes the platform, is about positioning. The research finding is about distribution. They are not the same decision and need not conflict: the product can be universal while the recruiting list is concentrated.

Expectations the market has already set

Users arrive at LINK with mental models formed elsewhere, and those models are now conventions rather than preferences. INFERENCE

From booking tools

Availability shown in my own time zone without being asked. A confirmation that arrives immediately. A calendar invitation. A one-tap join link. Rescheduling without contacting anyone.

From marketplaces

Ratings with enough volume to mean something. A visible refund policy before paying. Someone to contact when it goes wrong. Payment held until delivery.

From social platforms

A verified badge that means verified. Follower counts that are current. Profiles that look like the creator rather than like a directory listing.

Phase 1 meets most of these. The notable exceptions are self-serve rescheduling and calendar sync, both deferred to Phase 2, and both likely to generate support contacts during the pilot. That is an acceptable trade at thirty creators and an unacceptable one at three hundred. INFERENCE

What the comparison establishes

LINK's proposed 15 percent sits defensibly mid-pack, above Upwork and Patreon, below Fiverr, and well below Cameo and Intro's marketplace tier. Combined with a customer-side processing fee, "creators keep 85 percent" is both literally true and competitively strong. INFERENCE

Every comparator solves trust, and three of the five solve it by exclusion. Intro curates, Cameo trades on fame, Upwork and Fiverr use accumulated work history. LINK can use none of these at launch: it is open by design, its creators are not famous, and it has no history. INFERENCE That leaves verified follower count as effectively the only trust mechanism available on day one, which is why its absence from the prototype is the most consequential gap in the product.

Opportunity: the attribution-aware take rate

Intro charges less when the expert brought the customer and more when the platform did. That is the fairest logic available for a take rate and it maps precisely onto how LINK grows. Phase 1 should stay flat at 15 percent, because tiered attribution requires booking-source tracking that is not worth building yet, but the attribution data should be captured from day one. It costs very little during build and it is expensive to reconstruct later. It also gives Phase 2 a revenue lever that grows with the platform instead of taxing creators for their own audience. INFERENCE

The segmentation insight that should drive the design

Because the pilot customer arrives from a creator's own link, they are not comparing creators. They have already chosen. This changes the design problem fundamentally: the profile page is not a listing competing for attention in a marketplace, it is a conversion page for a decision mostly already made. INFERENCE

The implications are concrete. Browse and search matter far less than the proposal implies for Phase 1. The profile's job is to remove doubt and get to checkout fast, not to persuade from a standing start. Social proof matters less than proof of identity, because the visitor already believes in the person and needs to believe in the platform. That is a different trust problem and it is solved with payment protection and a clear refund policy, not with review counts the pilot cannot yet have.

Segment C — The administrator

One person, internal, operating the marketplace by hand. In Phase 1 this role is explicitly the substitute for trust and safety tooling, moderation queues, audit logs, dispute tooling and fraud screening, all of which are deferred. PROJECT RECORD

The capacity question has not been modelled anywhere in the project. At thirty creators and an unknown call volume, manual verification, manual refunds and manual suspension are plausible. INFERENCE The breaking point is unknown and should be watched rather than assumed. Section 27 proposes an operational load metric for exactly this. HYPOTHESIS

Behavioural analysis

Four behavioural dynamics that have direct design consequences in this product. Included only where they change a decision.

Borrowed trust transfers to the creator, not to the platformINFERENCE

The customer arrives trusting the creator completely and the platform not at all. These are separable and the design should treat them separately. Effort spent proving the creator is credible is largely wasted; effort spent proving LINK will not take the money and disappear is not. Design consequence: payment assurance, refund policy and processor branding earn their place near the pay button; creator testimonials and marketing copy do not.

Pricing doubt is the creator's emotional low pointHYPOTHESIS

Setting a price on your own time is a self-valuation, not a calculation, and it is the one onboarding step with no obviously correct answer. An empty field invites deliberation; deliberation invites abandonment. Design consequence: a suggested price with a stated basis converts an identity question into an acceptance-or-adjustment question, which is far easier. Showing the net figure alongside turns an abstract percentage into a concrete amount.

Loss aversion runs in opposite directions on each sideINFERENCE

The customer fears losing money to a no-show. The creator fears losing time to a cancellation. The approved cancellation policy addresses both, asymmetrically and deliberately: the customer is protected by held funds and refund windows, the creator by the two-hour no-refund cutoff. Design consequence: show each side the half of the policy that protects them, at the moment they feel the risk. The same policy text serves two different reassurance jobs.

Emptiness is read as failure, not as newnessEXTERNAL

A launching marketplace is structurally empty, and the cold-start literature identifies perceived density as the thing that makes a marketplace worth joining. A customer landing on a category with three creators and no reviews concludes the platform is dead, and does not return to check later. Design consequence: do not expose surfaces whose emptiness is visible. In the pilot, browse and categories should be demoted rather than featured, because the creator link is the route that works and the category page is the one that advertises how small the marketplace is.

Found

Affects

Why

Implication
Add SMS for the one-hour reminder only, the smallest possible addition at the highest-leverage moment. If declined, restate the gate threshold in writing before launch.

"Completed paid calls per week, trending up" is the right primary metric but it is vulnerable to a single marketing event.
