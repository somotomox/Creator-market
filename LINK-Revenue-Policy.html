01

How the money moves

Before any model can be assessed, the mechanics have to be clear, because the charge structure decides who carries the risk.

The money path, with where it can divert

Everything up to the payout is reversible. Once the creator has been paid, anything that goes wrong has to be recovered from someone. That is the entire argument for the review window and the payout threshold.

The customer's card is charged to LINK's account, not the creator's. Funds sit there until the call has happened and the review window has closed, then a transfer is made to the creator's connected account and paid out to their bank. In payment-processor terms this is the separate charges and transfers model, and it is the only one that supports holding funds.

This means LINK carries the chargeback liability, not the creator

Because LINK is the merchant of record, a customer who disputes a charge disputes it with LINK. The money comes back out of LINK's balance, plus a dispute fee, and if the creator has already been paid the platform is out of pocket until it can recover from them.

At pilot scale this is manageable and is the right trade, because the alternative (charging directly to the creator) makes held funds impossible and removes the product's main trust mechanism. But it should be a known position rather than a discovered one, and it is an argument for the review window before release.

The three moments that matter

Capture

The full amount is taken at booking, not at the call. A customer who has already paid is far more likely to attend, and an authorisation held for days risks expiring.

Hold

Funds sit with LINK through the call and the review window. This is what makes "your payment is held until the call is complete" a true statement rather than marketing.

Release

Transfer to the creator after the review window, then payout to their bank on the payout schedule. Two distinct steps, and the second one has its own cost.

The $2 monthly account fee is the one that distorts the model

It is a fixed cost attached to a creator, not a percentage of what they earn, and it only triggers when they actually get paid. That makes a dormant creator free and a barely-active creator expensive in relative terms. A creator doing one $15 call in a month generates about $2.24 of gross margin and costs $2.00 in account fees, which is close to break-even. Section 04 models this properly, and Section 07 proposes the policy that fixes it.

Note where the two Intro extremes sit. The same platform charges 10% or 50% for the identical service, and the only difference is who found the customer. In the pilot, every LINK booking is the 10% case.

The pattern across all five

The take rate tracks who supplies the demand. Upwork and Patreon sit at 10 percent because the creator brings the relationship. Fiverr and Cameo sit at 20 to 30 because the platform brings the buyer. Intro is the only one that makes this explicit by charging a different rate depending on the answer.

LINK's pilot is firmly in the first category. Every booking arrives through a creator's own shared link, which under Intro's logic is the 10 percent tier. At 15 percent, LINK is already charging above the going rate for the work it is actually doing, and that is worth knowing before anyone suggests raising it.

The model LINK is not using, and should not

A subscription model, where creators pay a monthly fee and keep everything, is the obvious alternative. Scheduling tools work this way. It is wrong for LINK for one specific reason: it charges creators before they have earned anything. A modest-following creator being asked for a monthly fee to find out whether their audience will pay for calls is being asked to take all of the risk. The commission model means LINK only earns when the creator earns, which is the correct alignment for an unproven category and is also the easier recruitment pitch.

The service fee does exactly what it was designed to do

LINK's effective take lands between 14.9 and 15.3 percent across the entire price band. That is the stated take rate, actually realised, which is unusual: in most marketplace models the headline rate and the real one drift apart because processing is absorbed somewhere.

Charging processing to the customer is what holds it flat. It is also what makes "creators keep 85 percent" literally true rather than approximately true.

A stated 15 percent becomes a realised 8 to 12 percent, and it is worst on the cheapest calls, which are the ones a pilot will see most of. The alternative is to take processing out of the creator's share, in which case they keep roughly 82 percent and every instance of the 85 percent claim has to be reworded, in marketing, on the profile, in onboarding and in the proposal itself.

05

Revenue structures assessed

Five structures, each judged against this specific business rather than in the abstract.

StructureHow it worksFor LINKVerdict
A · Flat commission
The current proposal
 A fixed percentage of every completed booking, with processing charged to the customer as a service fee. Simple to explain, simple to build, aligns platform income with creator income, and lands the effective take within a third of a percent of the stated one. Sits competitively between Upwork and Fiverr. Phase 1
B · Tiered by attribution
The Intro model
 A lower rate when the creator's own link brought the customer, higher when the platform's search or paid advertising did. The fairest possible logic, and the one that grows with the platform instead of taxing creators for their own audience. Needs booking-source tracking, and in the pilot nearly every booking is creator-sourced so there is nothing to tier yet. Phase 2
C · Subscription
The scheduling-tool model
 Creators pay a monthly fee and keep all or most of their earnings. Charges creators before they have earned anything, in a category where nobody yet knows whether audiences will pay. It puts all the risk on the person we are trying to recruit, and it makes the pitch far harder. Reject
D · Hybrid with a pro tier Commission for everyone, plus an optional paid tier buying lower commission, analytics or featured placement. Right eventually, wrong now. A pro tier only makes sense once there are creators earning enough that a lower rate is worth paying for, and once there is placement worth featuring on. Later
E · Loyalty taper The rate falls as a creator's lifetime volume rises, rewarding the ones who stay. Directly addresses the complaint creators have about flat-rate platforms, and it rewards exactly the behaviour the business model depends on, which is recurrence. But it is complex to communicate and premature before anyone has volume. Consider later

Why flat wins for the pilot and not forever

In the pilot, every booking arrives through a creator's own link. There is no marketplace demand to charge differently for, no volume history to taper against, and no featured placement to sell. A flat rate is not a compromise here, it is the only structure the available facts support.

What changes that is search, browse and paid acquisition actually bringing customers. The moment LINK is supplying demand rather than only infrastructure, structure B becomes available and it is the one to move to. That is why booking-source attribution should be captured from the first day even though nothing consumes it in Phase 1.

06

Recommended structure

Phase 1, for approval at the scope fix

  • 15 percent commission on every completed booking. Creators keep 85 percent.
  • Customer service fee of 3.5 percent plus $0.50, labelled as covering card processing, shown in the total at slot selection rather than at the card form.
  • Funds held from payment until the call completes and the review window closes.
  • Price band $15 to $250, with a suggested price shown rather than an empty field.
  • Payout threshold of $25, with weekly payouts above it. This is new and it is what protects the economics on low-value creators.
  • Booking-source attribution recorded from day one, unused until Phase 2.
  • Tips, if offered later, go entirely to the creator. Matching the strongest comparator convention and costing LINK almost nothing in the pilot.

The one new recommendation in that list

A $25 payout threshold was not in the original scope and it is worth its own paragraph. Without it, a creator earning $12.75 from a single call triggers a payout, which costs $0.25 plus 0.25 percent, and activates the $2 monthly account fee, leaving the platform 23 cents. With it, small balances accumulate until they are worth moving, which cuts both the payout count and the number of billable account-months.

It costs the creator nothing in money and a little in patience, and every comparable platform does some version of it. The creator-facing copy should say so plainly: "Payouts go out every week once your balance reaches $25."

The lever that matters most is not the take rate

Raising commission from 15 to 20 percent adds about $2 per $40 call and moves LINK from mid-pack to the top of the comparator range, which is where creator dissatisfaction has been documented. Raising the average call price from $40 to $75 adds $5.36 per call and costs nothing in goodwill.

The second is a product and positioning problem, not a pricing one, and it is where the attention should go.

The asymmetry is deliberate, and it should be explained rather than hidden

A customer cancelling late loses money. A creator cancelling late does not cost the customer anything. That looks uneven and it is, on purpose: the platform's credibility depends on the customer never being out of pocket for something the creator did. The service fee comes back too, which most platforms do not do, and it is a cheap way to make a bad experience survivable.

The cost of that policy is carried by the platform, not the creator, so it needs watching. If creator cancellations become common the policy stays and the creator gets suspended, rather than the policy changing.

Still open, and blocking

The no-show grace period is unspecified. Without it, there is no rule that says when a call counts as missed, which means the refund tier cannot be computed automatically and every disputed no-show is arbitrated by hand. Proposed: 10 minutes from the scheduled start, after which either party can mark the call as missed. This is questionnaire items Q16 and Q17 and it is on the critical path for the payment state machine.

7.4 Disputes and chargebacks

  • LINK is the merchant of record, so a disputed charge is disputed with LINK and the funds plus a dispute fee come out of LINK's balance.
  • If the creator has already been paid, the platform is out of pocket until it recovers. The review window and the payout threshold both reduce this exposure, which is a second reason for having them.
  • Evidence to retain for every booking: the booking record, both parties' join events from the video provider, the call duration, and any review. This is what a dispute is won or lost on, and it has to be captured at the time rather than reconstructed.
  • Repeat disputes against one creator are a suspension trigger, not a cost of doing business.
  • Phase 1 has no dispute tooling. Disputes are handled manually through the provider dashboard, which is acceptable at pilot volume and is on the Phase 2 list.

7.5 Tax and compliance

  • Creators are responsible for their own tax. LINK is not an employer and does not withhold.
  • The payment provider handles United States tax reporting for connected accounts, including identity collection and year-end forms where thresholds are met. This is one of the main reasons for using it rather than paying creators directly.
  • Phase 1 is United States and US dollars only, which keeps this simple. International payouts bring a materially larger compliance surface and belong after Phase 2.
  • Sales tax on the service fee has not been assessed. It is unlikely to apply to this transaction type in most states, but it is a question for the client's accountant rather than an assumption for us to make.
  • Terms, privacy and conduct policies are the client's legal responsibility under the agreement. The product links to them; it does not write them.

7.6 Changing the fees later

Worth settling now because it is cheap to agree and expensive to improvise. Any increase in the commission or the service fee should be announced to creators at least 30 days before it takes effect, should not apply to bookings already made, and should be visible in the creator dashboard rather than only emailed. Creators who discover a rate change after the fact leave, and they tell other creators.

What to take to the scope fix

  1. Approve the 15 percent commission and the customer service fee. The modelling shows it realises almost exactly the stated rate, and declining the fee costs a third of the margin.
  2. Settle the no-show grace period. Ten minutes, with the outcomes in 7.2. It unblocks the payment state machine.
  3. Approve the $25 payout threshold and weekly schedule. New, small, and it is what makes low-value creators viable.
  4. Confirm the review window at 24 hours. It sets when money releases and it is the main chargeback defence.
  5. Note the chargeback position. No action needed, but the client should know that LINK carries it.
